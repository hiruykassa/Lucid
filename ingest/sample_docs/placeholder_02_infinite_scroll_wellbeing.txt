Title: Infinite Scroll, Autoplay, and Self-Reported Digital Well-Being (placeholder)

This placeholder document stands in for a real paper on persuasive design features
and digital well-being. It exists so the ingestion pipeline can be developed and
tested before the actual ~50-paper corpus is curated.

Infinite scroll and autoplay are interface features that remove natural stopping
cues from a browsing or viewing session. Without an explicit endpoint, such as a
page break or an "are you still watching" prompt, users may continue consuming
content well past their original intention. Survey-based studies have associated
frequent exposure to these features with lower self-reported control over time
spent on an application, though causal claims are limited by the reliance on
cross-sectional, self-report methodology.

Some platforms have introduced opt-in interventions, such as scroll-end markers,
time-spent dashboards, or scheduled reminders, intended to restore a sense of
stopping points. Evaluations of these interventions show mixed effectiveness,
with usage reductions often modest and highly dependent on whether the
intervention is opt-in or set as a default.

Researchers studying digital well-being generally distinguish between design
features that are dark patterns in the deceptive or coercive sense and features
that are merely persuasive or attention-capturing without necessarily being
deceptive, though the boundary between these categories is actively debated.
